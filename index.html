The writing studio for people who like to write.

Binder, snapshots with side-by-side compare, cast and places, corkboard, timeline, research, and compile, over a folder of plain Markdown. Today that is an Obsidian plugin and a Scrivener importer, both open. Next is the Oakfall app for desktop, tablet, and phone, with sync and the editor tools as the one subscription. Everything is stored as Markdown, frontmatter, and git commits, so nothing needs Oakfall to be read.

Write, snapshot, compare, restore.

Before a risky edit, take a snapshot and give it a name. That is one git commit for one file, and you never see git. When the edit goes wrong, open the Snapshots tab: only this document's named snapshots are listed, not every auto-commit in the vault. Compare shows the old version beside the live text with changes tinted at the word level. Pull back a sentence, or restore the whole thing. Restore snapshots the current state first, so nothing is ever the last copy.

If you are coming from Scrivener, the importer replays every snapshot you ever took as history with its original title and date. You arrive with your past intact.

Arrange a chapter by thread.

This is Scrivener's corkboard arranged by label, and it is the view writers miss most. One thread per label: a point-of-view character, a plot line, a location. The chapter's scenes run down the axis in manuscript order, each card sitting on the thread that matches its label. Drag a card up or down and the scene moves in the book. Drag it sideways and its label changes. The picture is the order of events and who carries them, at a glance.

Pin research to the scene that needs it.

Attach a PDF, an image, or a note to the document you are writing and it shows up beside the text whenever that document is open. Highlight a passage in the PDF and the highlight links back to the exact sentence it supports. Research lives in the vault as ordinary files; a pin is one line of frontmatter.

Pin a scene to a place.

Any image can be a map: a hand-drawn world, an Inkarnate export, a scanned town plan, a floor plan of the house, or a blank canvas. Drop a pin and link it to a place, a scene, or a character. Draw on top of it in the same ink as this page: woods, water, hills, roads, rivers, routes, and labels, clean or hand-drawn, with a small toolbar that works the way drawing apps do. A place is a note, like a character, so Oakfall knows where it appears: hover a pin or a drawn region and you get the chapters and scenes set there, the line that names it, the images you pinned for inspiration, and a search box for when a place carries half the book. Click a scene to open it. Pins and drawings are stored on the map's own note as plain frontmatter, so the map is a picture with a readable list beside it, and Gall can read and write that list too.

Keep track of who is where.

A character is a note. Scrivener gives you a sheet to fill in; Oakfall gives you the sheet and then reads the manuscript for you. Every scene that links to the character, or mentions a name or alias you have approved, counts as an appearance. The cast panel shows each character across the book in manuscript order, where they were last seen, and which thread they carry on the corkboard. Click a cell to open the scene. Nothing is typed twice: the sheet is frontmatter, the appearances are links.

Meet Gall.

Every studio has someone who knows where things are. Gall is Oakfall's. It lives in the margin, knows your manuscript, and stays out of your prose. Ask it what changed in chapter four since the June draft, where Tom was last seen, which scenes still mention the tin after you cut it, or whether a synopsis still matches its scene. It answers from your files and your history, and anything it changes lands as an ordinary edit or a snapshot you can undo.

Half of Gall needs no model at all. Where someone was last seen, what is set at a place, who is in a scene, which places have no scene yet, how long a chapter is, where a note sits on a map: those are lookups over your files, and that half of Gall is open source, works offline, and ships with the plugin and the app. The half that reads your prose is the hosted one.

Gall is free, and off until you turn it on. For the half that reads your prose, bring your own key or point it at a local model; hosted models come later as a separate tier. It sends your manuscript nowhere you did not point it, and it reports nothing back to us.

What is open, and what is not.

Anything that touches your files is open source under Apache-2.0 and always will be, because nobody should trust a writing tool that can hold a manuscript hostage. What you can pay for is sync between your devices and the editor tools.

Open, Apache-2.0

  • The vault spec the contract everything reads and writes
  • The importer Scrivener in, vault and history out
  • The Obsidian plugin, its core, and its module and theme APIs
  • Snapshots, binder, inspector, corkboard and threads, research, cast, flat maps, targets
  • Themes and project templates an open format; build your own, share them, sell them

Paid

  • Sync end-to-end encrypted, between your desktop, tablet, and phone
  • The editor tools focus and audit, the editor's redlines back from Word, beta-reader round trips, compile presets
  • Hosted models for Gall later, as a separate tier

One subscription, $5 a month or $4 a month billed annually. The app itself is free, and a whole novel can be written in it without paying. Stop paying and your files are untouched: there is nothing in them that only a paid tool can read.

What changes in Obsidian

The file tree becomes a binder.
An ordered manuscript you drag to reorder. Folders are chapters, folder notes are chapter text.
The properties panel becomes an inspector.
Synopsis, label, status, notes, keywords, bookmarks, and attached research beside what you are writing.
Raw commits become named snapshots.
Per document, with compare and safe restore.
Three plugins become one board.
Grid, kanban columns, and label threads are arrangements of the same cards, reading the same synopsis, label, status, and date fields.
Research stays with the scene.
PDFs, images, and notes attached to the document you have open. Highlights link back.
Compile means a manuscript.
Presets for short story, novel, poem, essay. Docx, pdf, epub, or Markdown. Your files are untouched.
Any image becomes a map, flat or in relief.
Pins link places to scenes and back. Stored as frontmatter on the map's note.
Characters track themselves.
A character is a note; its appearances are the scenes that link to it or name it. The cast panel reads the book so you don't have to.
Every module has a switch.
One settings page. Turn off what you do not write with; nothing is stranded because every module reads the same fields.
Gall knows the book.
Ask what changed, who was where, what depends on what. Answers come from your files and history; changes land as edits you can undo.

Works with how you already sync

The plugin does not sync anything. Your vault keeps moving through iCloud, Dropbox, Obsidian Sync, Syncthing, or a git remote, whichever you use today. The Oakfall app adds its own end-to-end encrypted sync as the subscription, for writers who would rather not run one of those; the files stay plain either way. Snapshot history is stored as plain files inside the vault by default, so it travels with the vault to every device, including your phone. If you want real version control as well, turn on the git store: desktop only, and the repository is kept outside the synced folder so cloud sync never touches it.

Get it

Moving a project out of Scrivener

Our importer reads a .scriv package and writes a vault that follows the Oakfall spec, snapshots included. The original project is never modified.

go install github.com/oakfall/scrivener-to-obsidian/cmd/scriv2obsidian@latest
scriv2obsidian convert -out ~/Writing -git Novel.scriv

The plugin

In early development, and usable in a test vault. The importer is feature complete for its first release. The plugin core, the map with pins, place cards, and drawing, the New command, and Gall's offline answers build and pass their tests; nothing is published yet. The spec, the importer, the plugin, and every module that touches your files are open source and will stay that way. Sync and the editor tools are the paid part; nothing that guards your data ever will be.
